# SOC Metrics and Objectives (TryHackMe): Study Notes

## 1. Big Picture

A Security Operations Center (SOC) protects an organisation's **confidentiality, integrity, and availability (CIA)** by collecting telemetry, generating alerts, investigating suspicious activity, and coordinating response.

SOC metrics help answer four questions:

1. **How much work is arriving?** — Alerts Count (AC)
2. **How much of that work is noise?** — False Positive Rate (FPR)
3. **How often does L1 need help from L2?** — Alert Escalation Rate (AER)
4. **How reliably are attacks detected?** — Threat Detection Rate (TDR)

Time-based metrics measure how quickly the SOC detects, acknowledges, and responds to an incident:

- **MTTD:** Mean Time to Detect
- **MTTA:** Mean Time to Acknowledge
- **MTTR:** Mean Time to Respond (using this room's definition)

Metrics are useful only when their definitions, time boundaries, data sources, and reporting periods are consistent. A metric is an indicator, not a complete assessment of SOC quality.

## 2. Core Operational Metrics

| Metric | Formula | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Alerts Count (AC) | Total alerts received in a defined period | Overall SOC workload |
| False Positive Rate (FPR) | False Positives / Total Alerts × 100 | Proportion of alerts that are false positives |
| Alert Escalation Rate (AER) | Escalated Alerts / Total Alerts × 100 | Proportion of alerts escalated by L1 |
| Threat Detection Rate (TDR) | Detected Threats / Total Threats × 100 | Detection coverage against known threats |

### 2.1 Alerts Count (AC)

**Definition:** Total number of alerts received during a defined period.

- A high count may indicate increased malicious activity, noisy rules, duplicated alerts, or a change in telemetry.
- A very low count may be normal, but can also indicate broken ingestion, disabled rules, or poor visibility.
- The room gives **5–30 alerts per day per L1 analyst** as a general guideline. It is not a universal industry standard or a target that every SOC should force itself to meet.

**L1 actions**
- Check whether the increase is concentrated in one rule, host, user, or source IP.
- Identify duplicate or recurring alerts.
- Check SIEM/EDR ingestion health if alert volume unexpectedly drops.
- Report sustained queue growth and SLA risk to the shift lead.

**Important:** Low alert volume does not prove that the environment is secure.

### 2.2 False Positive Rate (FPR)

**Definition:** The percentage of alerts classified as False Positive.

\[
FPR = \frac{False\ Positives}{Total\ Alerts} \times 100
\]

**Example**

- Total alerts = 80
- False positives = 75
- FPR = (75 / 80) × 100 = **93.75%**

A high FPR creates alert noise and can contribute to **alert fatigue**: analysts become less attentive and may overlook genuine threats.

The room describes 80% or higher as a serious problem and 0% as an unrealistic ideal. Treat these as learning guidance, not universal thresholds.

**L1 actions**
1. Record which rules generate repeated false positives.
2. Capture the evidence and explain why the activity is benign.
3. Identify legitimate recurring activity, such as approved system updates.
4. Send rule-tuning recommendations to the detection/SOC engineering team.
5. Validate any proposed exclusion against known malicious cases before deployment.

**Caution:** Do not create broad exclusions just to lower FPR. An exclusion that suppresses malicious behavior creates a detection blind spot.

### 2.3 Alert Escalation Rate (AER)

**Definition:** The percentage of alerts escalated from L1 to a higher tier, such as L2.

\[
AER = \frac{Escalated\ Alerts}{Total\ Alerts} \times 100
\]

**Example**

- Total alerts = 120
- Escalated alerts = 24
- AER = (24 / 120) × 100 = **20%**

The room gives below 50%, ideally below 20%, as a target. These are not universal performance standards.

**Interpretation**
- A high AER may indicate that L1 needs more training, better workbooks, or clearer decision criteria. It may also reflect genuinely complex alerts or a serious threat campaign.
- A low AER may indicate good L1 capability, but it may also indicate that analysts are failing to escalate alerts they do not understand.

**L1 actions**
- Follow the relevant workbook/runbook.
- Escalate when evidence indicates a likely threat, impact is high, or the case is outside L1 authority.
- Record the reason for escalation and the evidence gathered.
- Ask for help when uncertain; do not suppress escalation merely to improve a KPI.

**Important:** AER is not a standalone measure of analyst skill. Review escalation quality and outcomes, not just the percentage.

### 2.4 Threat Detection Rate (TDR)

**Definition:** The proportion of known attacks/threats that the SOC detected.

\[
TDR = \frac{Detected\ Threats}{Total\ Known\ Threats} \times 100
\]

**Example**

- Known attacks = 6
- Detected attacks = 4
- TDR = (4 / 6) × 100 = **66.67%**

The room presents 100% as the desired result because every missed threat can have serious consequences. In practice, 100% detection cannot be assumed or guaranteed.

**Important limitations**
- The denominator must be known. In a real environment, undetected attacks may never be counted.
- Define what counts as a threat and what counts as detected.
- Detection is not the same as prevention or containment. An attack can be detected but still succeed.

**L1 actions**
- Report missed detections and suspicious activity that produced no alert.
- Preserve evidence and provide it to the detection engineering team.
- Identify the missing telemetry or rule condition.
- Retest the updated detection against both malicious and benign examples.

## 3. SLA and Time-Based Metrics

### 3.1 Service Level Agreement (SLA)

An **SLA (Service Level Agreement)** documents the service expectations between an internal SOC and its organisation, or between a Managed Security Service Provider (MSSP) and its customer.

For SOC operations, an SLA may define:
- SOC availability and coverage hours.
- Maximum time to detect or acknowledge alerts.
- Response targets by severity.
- Escalation paths and responsibilities.

An SLA must be read using the organisation's own definitions. Different teams may calculate similarly named metrics differently.

### 3.2 SOC Team Availability

The room gives **24/7** as a common availability example.

- **8/5:** 8 hours per day, 5 days per week.
- **24/7:** continuous coverage, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.

Availability describes the coverage schedule; it does not mean every alert is resolved instantly.

### 3.3 Mean Time to Detect (MTTD)

**Room definition:** Average time between the start of an attack and its detection by SOC tools.

\[
MTTD = \frac{\sum (Detection\ Time - Attack\ Start\ Time)}{Number\ of\ Detected\ Incidents}
\]

Example:

| Incident | Attack started | Detected | Detection time |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 09:00 | 09:04 | 4 min |
| B | 10:00 | 10:08 | 8 min |
| C | 11:00 | 11:03 | 3 min |

MTTD = (4 + 8 + 3) / 3 = **5 minutes**

MTTD is mainly affected by telemetry coverage, log ingestion delay, detection logic, and alert-generation speed. It is not the time an L1 analyst takes to open an alert.

### 3.4 Mean Time to Acknowledge (MTTA)

**Room definition:** Average time between a new alert being generated/received and an L1 analyst starting triage.

\[
MTTA = \frac{\sum (Triage\ Start - Alert\ Received)}{Number\ of\ Alerts}
\]

Example:

| Alert | Received | Triage started | Acknowledgement time |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 10:00 | 10:03 | 3 min |
| B | 10:10 | 10:20 | 10 min |
| C | 10:30 | 10:35 | 5 min |

MTTA = (3 + 10 + 5) / 3 = **6 minutes**

MTTA measures how quickly an analyst takes ownership and begins triage. It does not measure how long the full investigation takes.

### 3.5 Mean Time to Respond (MTTR)

**Room-specific definition:** Average time from the SOC receiving an alert until the breach is stopped. Under this definition, MTTR **includes MTTA**.

\[
MTTR = \frac{\sum (Breach\ Stopped - Alert\ Received)}{Number\ of\ Incidents}
\]

Example:

- Alert received: 11:00
- Breach stopped: 12:20
- Response time = 80 minutes

For this incident, MTTR = **80 minutes**.

**Terminology warning:** In the wider industry, MTTR can mean Mean Time to Respond, Recover, or Repair depending on the organisation. Always use the definition in the applicable SLA. This note follows the TryHackMe room's definition: alert received → breach stopped.

### 3.6 Timeline: How the Metrics Relate

\[
Attack\ Starts \rightarrow Detection \rightarrow Alert\ Received \rightarrow L1\ Starts\ Triage \rightarrow Breach\ Stopped
\]

| Stage | Metric | Start → End |
|---|---|---|
| Detection | MTTD | Attack starts → SOC tools detect it |
| Acknowledgement | MTTA | Alert received → L1 starts triage |
| Response | MTTR | Alert received → breach is stopped |

MTTR includes the acknowledgement period in this room's model. MTTD is measured before alert acknowledgement.

### 3.7 Common SLA Values from the Room

| Metric | Example value in the room |
|---|---:|
| SOC Team Availability | 24/7 |
| MTTD | 5 minutes |
| MTTA | 10 minutes |
| MTTR | 60 minutes |

These are **illustrative common SLA values from the learning material**, not mandatory limits for every SOC. Real targets depend on severity, risk, staffing, tooling, and contractual requirements.

## 4. Improving SOC Metrics

| Metric / Problem | Possible improvement | L1 contribution |
|---|---|---|
| FPR above 80% | Tune noisy rules; consider carefully scoped exclusions; automate repetitive low-risk triage | Identify recurring false positives and submit evidence-based tuning requests |
| MTTD above 30 min | Review detection-rule execution and log collection latency | Report missing/delayed telemetry and examples of missed detections |
| MTTA above 30 min | Real-time notifications; distribute queue workload more evenly | Monitor queue, acknowledge promptly, follow shift handover |
| MTTR above 4 hours | Improve escalation, documented playbooks, and response coordination | Escalate promptly and provide a concise evidence package to L2 |

### Practical cautions

- **Rule tuning:** A detection engineer should validate changes against malicious and benign test data.
- **Automation:** SOAR or custom scripts can enrich and route repetitive alerts, but high-impact decisions should retain appropriate human oversight.
- **Queue management:** Prioritise according to severity, asset criticality, confidence, and SLA deadlines—not simply alert age or volume.
- **Escalation:** L1 should not delay escalation while attempting actions outside their permissions.
- **Playbooks:** Procedures should identify decision points, required evidence, owners, and authorised containment actions.

## 5. Practical L1 Workflow: Notice → Investigate → Communicate

The room's practical task emphasises that metrics tracking is often owned by SOC management, but technical analysts should recognise operational problems and communicate improvements.

When you notice a metric degrading:

1. **Identify the symptom**
   - Queue volume rising?
   - Repeated false positives from one rule?
   - Alerts waiting too long for acknowledgement?
   - Incidents taking too long to contain?
   - Known attack activity not generating alerts?

2. **Collect evidence**
   - Reporting period and affected alert/incident IDs.
   - Rule name, severity, host, user, and relevant timestamps.
   - Number of alerts reviewed, false positives, and escalations.
   - Log ingestion delays or missing data, where relevant.

3. **Check the metric definition**
   - Confirm numerator and denominator.
   - Confirm which timestamps are used.
   - Use the same reporting window and classification criteria.
   - Do not compare two teams using different MTTR or FPR definitions as if they were identical.

4. **Propose a controlled improvement**
   - Rule tuning request.
   - Telemetry/ingestion investigation.
   - Queue distribution or notification improvement.
   - Workbook update or analyst training.
   - SOAR automation proposal with safeguards.

5. **Validate the result**
   - Compare before/after values over comparable periods.
   - Check that true threats are still detected.
   - Check for new blind spots or unintended side effects.

### Example issue report

> **Issue:** The endpoint detection rule generates a high volume of repeated alerts classified as false positives.
>
> **Evidence:** During the reviewed period, 75 of 80 alerts were classified as false positives (FPR: 93.75%).
>
> **Impact:** High alert noise may increase analyst workload and the risk of overlooking actionable alerts.
>
> **Recommendation:** Review the rule conditions and identify the recurring benign process/activity. Test a narrowly scoped tuning change against known malicious and benign events before deployment.
>
> **Validation:** Compare alert volume and FPR after the change, while confirming that representative malicious test cases still trigger the detection.

This is a sample report based on the room's 75/80 example, not a claim about a real SOC environment.

## 6. Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Treating low alert count as proof of security | SIEM ingestion or detection may be broken | Check telemetry health and detection coverage |
| Trying to force FPR to 0% | Some false positives are expected; broad exclusions can hide threats | Tune carefully and validate |
| Treating low AER as automatically good | L1 may be failing to escalate uncertain or serious cases | Review escalation quality and missed escalations |
| Assuming TDR is easy to calculate | The total number of attacks may be unknown | Define a test set or use known incident data |
| Confusing detection with prevention | A detected attack may still succeed | Track detection and containment separately |
| Mixing MTTD and MTTA | One measures detection; the other measures analyst acknowledgement | Use the correct start/end timestamps |
| Using MTTR without defining it | Respond, Recover, and Repair are used differently | Follow the SLA's exact definition |
| Excluding trusted activity without testing | A broad exclusion can suppress malicious behavior | Scope, test, approve, and monitor rule changes |
| Optimising KPIs at the expense of security | Analysts may close alerts or avoid escalation to improve numbers | Evaluate evidence quality and outcomes, not only speed |

## 8. Quick Revision Notes

- **AC:** How many alerts arrived?
- **FPR:** What percentage of alerts were false positives?
- **AER:** What percentage of alerts were escalated?
- **TDR:** What percentage of known threats were detected?
- **MTTD:** Attack started → SOC tools detected it.
- **MTTA:** Alert received → L1 started triage.
- **MTTR (this room):** Alert received → breach stopped; includes MTTA.
- High FPR can cause alert fatigue.
- Low alert volume may indicate missing visibility.
- Low AER is not automatically good; under-escalation is possible.
- TDR requires a known total number of threats.
- Detection does not equal prevention or containment.
- SLA targets and metric definitions vary between organisations.
- Never manipulate a KPI by closing alerts carelessly or creating unsafe exclusions.

## 9. Self-Test

1. A SOC receives 200 alerts and classifies 150 as false positives. What is the FPR?
2. Out of 120 alerts, L1 escalates 24. What is the AER?
3. An attack begins at 09:00 and SOC tools detect it at 09:12. What is the MTTD for this incident?
4. An alert is received at 10:00 and L1 begins triage at 10:18. What is the acknowledgement time?
5. An alert is received at 11:00 and the breach is stopped at 12:20. What is the MTTR under this room's definition?
6. AER is very low, but analysts close alerts they do not understand without evidence. What is the likely problem?
7. Why can a TDR of 100% be difficult to prove in a real environment?
8. Why is creating a broad exclusion to reduce FPR dangerous?

1. (150 / 200) × 100 = **75%**.
2. (24 / 120) × 100 = **20%**.
3. **12 minutes**.
4. **18 minutes**.
5. **80 minutes**.
6. Possible **under-escalation**. Low AER does not prove good triage.
7. The total number of attacks, including attacks that were never detected, may be unknown.
8. It may suppress malicious activity that matches the excluded pattern, creating a detection blind spot.
